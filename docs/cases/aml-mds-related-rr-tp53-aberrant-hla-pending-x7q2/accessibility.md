## Allograft Hct2 interventions (4)

### 1. Second allogeneic HCT for relapse after first allo-HCT <small><code>HCT2, second allo-HCT, HSCT2</code></small> { #access-1 }

**Access status:** <span class="fit-badge fit-strong">Standard of care</span> &nbsp; **Modality:** other &nbsp; **Verified:** 2026-07-06


A second allo-HCT is the curative-intent platform this whole plan sits on, and it is standard of care rather than anything experimental. It is also the hardest to earn: registry data put 2-year survival around 37% in matched-sibling second transplants, better with the patient&#x27;s long ~6-year first remission but worse with 85% marrow blasts and adverse cytogenetics going in. The gate is disease control and fitness, not access.


<dl class="profile-grid">
  <dt>Regulatory</dt><dd>established transplant procedure; no drug approval involved</dd>
  <dt>Guidelines</dt><dd>Recognized option for relapse after a first allo-HCT (ASTCT/EBMT/NCCN transplant guidance)</dd>
  <dt>Geographic scope</dt><dd>Any allo-HCT transplant center.</dd>
</dl>


**Next steps**

1. Get a second-transplant consult at the patient&#x27;s allo-HCT center to confirm candidacy (organ function, cardiac, goals of care).
2. Settle donor strategy: same HLA-identical sibling versus an alternative donor, informed by the HA-1/HA-2 genotyping already underway.
3. Sequence cytoreduction to get the marrow as close to remission as possible before HCT2, since disease burden at transplant is the dominant survival driver.


**Trial pathways**

_No registered trials in the dossier._


**Manufacturer / sponsor contact**

<dl class="profile-grid">
  <dt>Notes</dt><dd>Procedure delivered at an allo-HCT transplant center; no manufacturer.</dd>
</dl>


**Payer / coverage notes.** Covered as a standard transplant procedure when a patient meets center and insurer criteria (disease control, organ function, donor availability). A second transplant draws closer payer scrutiny than the first, so pre-authorization and a center consult come early.

### 3. Donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI) from the sibling donor <small><code>DLI, donor lymphocyte infusion</code></small> { #access-3 }

**Access status:** <span class="fit-badge fit-strong">Standard of care</span> &nbsp; **Modality:** other &nbsp; **Verified:** 2026-07-06


Sibling DLI is standard of care and readily available given the matched-sibling donor, but the honest expectation is modest. Registry data favor DLI when relapse comes late (this patient at ~6 years qualifies) and when it is given in remission, yet with 85% marrow blasts and adverse cytogenetics the active-disease subgroup sees roughly 15% 2-year survival. That argues for cytoreduction before DLI rather than DLI into florid disease.


<dl class="profile-grid">
  <dt>Regulatory</dt><dd>established post-transplant procedure; no drug approval involved</dd>
  <dt>Guidelines</dt><dd>Recognized for AML relapse after allo-HCT (ASTCT/EBMT practice)</dd>
  <dt>Geographic scope</dt><dd>The patient&#x27;s allo-HCT center, using the original sibling donor.</dd>
</dl>


**Next steps**

1. Confirm the sibling donor is available and willing to donate lymphocytes again.
2. Cytoreduce toward remission before DLI rather than infusing into high-burden disease, and pair DLI with the second-transplant plan rather than using it as a standalone salvage.
3. Watch for GVHD, the characteristic DLI toxicity, especially if combined with HLA-restricted TCR-T consolidation.


**Trial pathways**

_No registered trials in the dossier._


**Manufacturer / sponsor contact**

<dl class="profile-grid">
  <dt>Notes</dt><dd>Cells collected from the original sibling donor at the transplant center; no manufacturer.</dd>

**Payer / coverage notes.** Covered as a standard post-transplant procedure. Access hinges on the sibling donor being available and willing to donate lymphocytes again.

### 4. FLAMSA-RIC sequential chemotherapy + reduced-intensity allo-HCT (+ prophylactic DLI) <small><code>FLAMSA-RIC, sequential FLAMSA conditioning</code></small> { #access-4 }

FLAMSA-RIC is a way to take refractory AML with adverse cytogenetics straight into a reduced-intensity transplant, and it is standard of care where centers offer it. It is the sequencing this patient&#x27;s plan is built on, and it competes with Iomab-B conditioning for the same job. Access comes down to picking a transplant center that does it.


<dl class="profile-grid">

  <dt>Guidelines</dt><dd>Used for high-risk/refractory AML at transplant centers, mainly European practice</dd>
  <dt>Geographic scope</dt><dd>Transplant centers offering the regimen (more common in Europe).</dd>
</dl>


**Next steps**

1. Ask the transplant center whether it runs FLAMSA-RIC sequencing and how it compares, for this patient, with targeted Iomab-B conditioning.
2. Fold the prophylactic-DLI component into the post-transplant plan alongside any HLA-restricted TCR-T consolidation.


**Trial pathways**
